# Move 1: all-future prefix surplus from finite-rank equation finiteness

Date: 2026-09-07. Source: node-000005, move 1, pass 1.
Strategy: reduce-and-translate, step 2.
Entry: change-the-ambient-space.
Status: analytical proof candidate of the exact admitted auxiliary claim.
It is not yet independently accepted. The original coverage problem remains open.

## Exact statement

For every finite set D of distinct integers at least 3 with gcd(D)=1 and sigma(D)=sum_{d in D}1/(d-1)>=1, every integer k>=1, and every finite set F of labelled occurrences (d,j) with d in D and j>=k, list the remaining occurrences by increasing value and then increasing base label as a_1,a_2,... and put S_m=sum_{i=1}^m a_i; then S_m-a_{m+1} tends to positive infinity as m tends to infinity.

Here a labelled occurrence is a pair (d,j). Distinct pairs with the same
numerical value are separate terms. No finite threshold is computed.

## Candidate images and chosen translation

The relation to control is S_m-a_{m+1}>B eventually for each fixed B.
There are three useful descriptions of the residual relation.

1. Real ordered next-power gaps are nonnegative. This reproduces the
   predecessor's constant lower bound at reciprocal mass one and does
   not by itself prove divergence.
2. Bounded differences of two selected pure powers define finitely many
   fixed-coefficient linear incidences in a finite-rank multiplicative
   subgroup. This retains the power structure and admits the theorem below.
3. A near equality of two powers yields a small linear form in logarithms.
   Quantitative tools of this type appear in BEGL96, p.137. An effective
   estimate is unnecessary for the present qualitative claim.

We use the second image. We do not assert finiteness for arbitrary real
ordered pairs or for equal powers. The multiplicative group and the
separate exclusion of equality are essential to the pull-back.

## Imported theorem and exact application

The finite-rank theorem of Evertse, Schlickewei and Schmidt states that
a fixed linear equation with nonzero coefficients, in a finite-rank
subgroup of the multiplicative torus over an algebraically closed
characteristic-zero field, has finitely many nondegenerate solutions.
Nondegenerate means that no nonempty proper subsum vanishes. The full
sum is one and is therefore nonzero as well.

Reference: J.-H. Evertse, H. P. Schlickewei and W. M. Schmidt,
*Linear Equations in Variables which Lie in a Multiplicative Group*,
Annals of Mathematics 155 (2002), DOI 10.2307/3062133,
arXiv:math/0409604v1, main theorem in Section 1 (Theorem 1.1 in the
paper's internal references, displayed as 0.1 by the saved HTML conversion).
The exact theorem statement and hypotheses were inspected. This proof
uses that published theorem, not a new proof of it or a formal certificate.

Fix integers a,b>=3 and a nonzero integer c. In K=the algebraic closure
of Q, consider

    Gamma = { (a^u,b^v) : u,v in Z } subset (K^*)^2.

This group is generated by (a,1) and (1,b). These two generators are
independent: (a^u,b^v)=(1,1) implies u=v=0, since a,b>1. Thus Gamma has
finite rank two. The equation

    (1/c) X - (1/c) Y = 1                                      (1)

has fixed nonzero coefficients and is equivalent to X-Y=c.
Every solution in Gamma is nondegenerate: its two singleton summands
are nonzero, and their sum is one. The imported theorem implies that
(1) has finitely many solutions in Gamma. The map from exponent pairs
to (a^u,b^v) is injective. Consequently,

    for each nonzero integer c, a^u-b^v=c
    has only finitely many pairs of positive integers (u,v).   (2)

No coprimality or multiplicative independence between a and b is
required for (2). The possible zero difference has not been covered.

## A pair whose positive powers never coincide

Since sigma(D)>=1, D is nonempty. Choose a in D and a prime p dividing a.
Because gcd(D)=1, some b in D is not divisible by p. In particular b!=a.
For all positive u,v, the integer a^u is divisible by p and b^v is not.
Therefore a^u!=b^v.

For any real H>=0, only finitely many nonzero integers c satisfy |c|<=H.
Taking the union of the finite sets in (2), and using the exclusion of
zero difference, proves that

    { (u,v) in Z_{>=1}^2 : |a^u-b^v|<=H } is finite.           (3)

In particular, along any choice of exponent pairs for which both
a^u and b^v tend to infinity, their absolute difference tends to
infinity. To see the eventual, rather than subsequential, conclusion,
let R_H be the maximum of the two power values in all pairs in (3),
with R_H=0 when there is no such pair. Once both values exceed R_H,
their difference exceeds H. Existence of this finite maximum follows
from finiteness; this argument provides no effective way to locate it.

The proof uses collective gcd one only to choose this one pair.
It makes no claim that every pair of bases is coprime or has disjoint
prime support.

## Exact prefix identity, including deletions and ties

Fix D,k,F. There are infinitely many remaining occurrences and only
finitely many of value at most any fixed real number. Indeed, each
of the finitely many bases has that property, and F is finite.
Thus the ordered remaining stream is defined at every index, and

    q_m := a_{m+1} tends to infinity.                          (4)

Put

    C = sum_{d in D} d^k/(d-1) + sum_{(d,j) in F} d^j.

For m sufficiently large, q_m exceeds every value deleted by F.
The condition is automatic for all m when F is empty if its maximum
is interpreted as zero.

For each d in D, let x_d(m)=d^{ell_d(m)} be the first remaining
occurrence of that base not used in the first m terms. Then

    x_d(m)>=q_m for every d, and min_{d in D} x_d(m)=q_m.       (5)

The prefix contains every nondeleted power of base d below x_d(m),
and no nondeleted power of that base at or above x_d(m).
Every deleted power is below q_m and hence below x_d(m).
The contribution of base d to the prefix is therefore exactly

    (x_d(m)-d^k)/(d-1) - sum_{(d,j) in F} d^j.

The subtraction in this line is restricted to the indicated base d.
Summing over the bases gives

    S_m = sum_{d in D} x_d(m)/(d-1) - C.

Writing sigma=sum_{d in D}1/(d-1) and subtracting q_m yields

    S_m-q_m
      = (sigma-1)q_m
        + sum_{d in D} (x_d(m)-q_m)/(d-1) - C.                (6)

This computation is per labelled base stream. If the prefix ends
among equal numerical values, the streams whose occurrence has not
yet been taken have next value q_m, while those already processed
advance to their next power. Formulae (5) and (6) remain exact.
Coincident values are never identified or discarded.

## Pull-back to the entire surplus limit

Use the pair a,b selected above, and put M=max(a,b).
Let

    W_m = sum_{d in D} (x_d(m)-q_m)/(d-1).

All terms in W_m are nonnegative. Let d_* be whichever of a,b has
the larger next power at this prefix. Since q_m is no larger than
the smaller of those two powers, and d_*-1<=M-1,

    W_m >= (x_{d_*}(m)-q_m)/(d_*-1)
        >= |x_a(m)-x_b(m)|/(M-1).                            (7)

By (4) and (5), both x_a(m) and x_b(m) tend to infinity. Their
exponents are at least k>=1. Statement (3) therefore implies

    |x_a(m)-x_b(m)| tends to infinity.                       (8)

Finally, sigma>=1 and q_m>0. Combining (6)-(8),

    S_m-a_{m+1}
      >= |x_a(m)-x_b(m)|/(M-1) - C
      tends to positive infinity.

For clarity, given any real B, choose
H=max(0,(M-1)(B+C)). For all sufficiently large m, all deletions
are below q_m and the gap in (8) exceeds H. The displayed lower
bound then exceeds B. This establishes the claimed order of
quantifiers for every input D,k,F and every real B.

This proof covers both strict reciprocal mass and equality mass.
For sigma>1 the simpler lower bound
S_m-a_{m+1}>=(sigma-1)a_{m+1}-C already proves divergence.
At equality, (7)-(8) supply the previously missing unbounded term.
